## Stage 1: The problem (Part I, judged on its own)

**Short answer:** Partly real. The rent is real, large and well sourced. The control concern is plausible but looks ahead rather than describing today. The "duplicated agent labour" problem is the weakest part: it is unmeasured and partly double-counts the rent. I score its importance 57/100, with medium confidence.

### 1. Is it real? Partly

Take the paper's own example, the lead-to-invoice workflow in a sales business. It really does run through a rented CRM such as Salesforce, and money really is spent on seats that aren't used.

The paper misses a conceptual tension, though. **SaaS already is "build once, reuse everywhere."** A company renting Salesforce is not rebuilding a CRM. The rent problem and the duplication problem are largely the same thing seen from two sides: you either rent (no duplication, but lock-in) or you build (duplication). Real duplication sits mostly in custom glue, integrations and internal tools, which a shared catalogue may not capture well because that work is specific to each company.

### 2. How big is it?

| Item | Paper (low / central / high) | My view |
|---|---|---|
| In-scope rent | $57 / 90 / 120bn | **Agree**: $60–130bn, central about $90bn. A1 (the category share) is the only driver, and its floor is sourced. But rent pays for hosting, security, compliance, support and accountability. The part you could actually remove (vendor margin, sales and marketing, unused seats) is roughly 30–50% of it, about **$25–45bn**. |
| Unused licences | $14 / 32 / 55bn | I'd **lower the floor** to about 20% unused, giving $12–50bn with a central near $27bn. Zylo's customers are self-selected, and "unused" usually means inactive during a period, not truly waste. |
| Agent coding compute (list price) | $3.6 / 31 / 165bn | **Plausible**, maybe slightly low given 2026 growth. I'd say $15–80bn, central about $35bn. The high corner is not meaningful. |
| Duplicated business-app compute | $0.1 / 3.7 / 45bn | **Lower**: $0.5–8bn, central about $2bn. A7 should mean work a shared catalogue would *actually have prevented*, not code that merely resembles code elsewhere. |
| Power | 0.1 / 4 / 66 TWh | **Agree** it is small: about 1–10 TWh for all agent coding. It should carry no weight, and the paper says so itself. |
| Ceiling saving | $54bn a year after migration | This assumes 100% of in-scope rent moves. A realistic long-run share for open alternatives is 1–5%, or about **$1–5bn a year**. |

The assumptions that dominate are A1 (rent), A6 and A7 (compute), and migration cost E3 (enterprise savings). I would add a missing one: whether the cost of writing code is the barrier at all. Open-source CRM, ERP, chat and e-signature tools already exist (Odoo, ERPNext, Twenty, SuiteCRM, Mattermost, Zulip, Chatwoot, Documenso), and they have not displaced SaaS. That points to distribution, trust, migration and support as the real bottleneck.

### 3. Importance for humanity: 57/100, medium confidence

| Dimension | Score /20 | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Impact | 12 | Tens of billions in rent, but much of it buys real services. The control concern is serious if it plays out. |
| Breadth | 15 | Nearly every business uses SaaS, and agent adoption is broad. |
| Urgency / irreversibility | 11 | Lock-in to the layer agents act through is forming now. But open standards (MCP, the protocol agents use to connect to tools and data) and regulation such as the EU Data Act's switching rules push against it. |
| Evidence | 9 | Rent is sourced. Duplication is unmeasured, which the paper admits. Control is a forecast. |
| Tractability | 10 | AI lowers the cost of building, and open alternatives exist. The barriers that remain are mostly non-technical. |

### 4. The four theses

- **Control** (importance 8/10, compelling 6/10, medium confidence). This is the strongest thesis. The layer agents act through does gather identity, permissions and memory together. It would be false if agent tooling standardises openly (MCP is early evidence of that), regulation keeps exit cheap, or open-weight models stay close to frontier quality (they largely are).
- **Efficiency** (5, 4, medium). This is weak as argued. SaaS already removes duplication, and the duplicated share is assumed. If regenerating code keeps getting cheaper, a "cache" of code is worth *less*, not more; the real value would be in verified, maintained quality, which the paper under-argues. It would be false if reuse per capability stays low, or if overhead exceeds what reuse saves. The paper's own low case shows exactly that.
- **Future of software engineering** (7, 6, medium). The direction is widely shared and reasonable, and the paper's framing ("specify and verify is the bottleneck") is good. It would be false if review cannot keep pace, or if writing a correct spec costs about as much as writing the code.
- **Contribution primitive** (5, 3, low). The claim that no open layer gets built at scale without such a primitive is contradicted by Linux, Postgres and Kubernetes. Those were built by employment, foundations and commercial open-source companies. Funded bounties and salaried maintainers directing agents are simpler candidates. It is already false if those mechanisms keep working when agents do the labour, and I think they probably will.

## Stage 2: The approach (Part II, judged against Stage 1)

**Short answer:** It is not an efficient attack on the problem as it actually is. The effort goes into reward accounting, token design and governance, while the real bottleneck is adoption: a product people trust, migration into it, support and compliance. The paper is unusually honest, and the coordinator code is real. But no product exists, it has one contributor, and the key protocol cannot be inspected. Effectiveness 15/100, credibility 25/100, readiness: prototype (the coordinator only; the product and reward protocol are concepts).

### 5. Is it well targeted, and what share could it address?

It aims at the right money (the rent). It then puts its heaviest machinery, two maximum-reasoning agent reviews plus a human, audits and consensus rounds, where the paper's own model shows savings are most fragile. Migration, the dominant enterprise cost, has no design yet (Amendment 03 is unwritten).

The paper's four conditions:
- **(a) Reuse outweighs overhead:** unknown. Plausible for generic capabilities, but break-even needs 2.5–5 reusers. Measure it as reuse per catalogue feature against review compute per feature.
- **(b) Verification is cheaper than regenerating:** unmeasured, and doubtful for complex features under this review load. Measure human minutes and review compute per accepted unit.
- **(c) Contributing pays better than free-riding:** fails today, by design. The token has no value.
- **(d) The open layer is chosen, and exit is real:** cannot be tested, because there is no product.

My share estimate for five years out is 0–0.1% of in-scope rent (at most about $100m a year), with a central near zero. Duplicated compute captured: close to zero. That would move up if a narrow module shipped end to end and gained paying organisations, and if independent contributors got work accepted.

### 6. Credibility: 25/100

- **For:**
  - It is candid: it labels claims as BUILT, DESIGNED or PROPOSED, admits every target is at 0%, and says its own low case loses money.
  - The model is reproducible, and I ran it.
  - There is substantial real code with named adversarial tests.
  - It includes falsification criteria and a fair section on related work.
- **Against:**
  - A single founder, whose public history is about a day old.
  - No product, and no real agent build under the protocol yet.
  - The protocol is unpublished, and the paper itself reports two "do not implement" verdicts from its outside AI reviewer.
  - It targets Salesforce parity on every surface.
  - No importers exist.
  - It depends on contributors' consumer AI subscriptions, which may not permit this use.
  - It builds a lot of token and governance machinery for zero users.

### 7. Readiness

It is a prototype of the coordinator: code, tests and a live site and API. The product, the reward protocol and the token are concepts or unpublished drafts. Nothing is released.

### 8. The edge cases it considered

- **Handled (on main, with named tests that I found in the repo but didn't run):** lease races, self-review, sealed verdicts, forged submissions, verdicts replayed against a different diff, row-level security, append-only records.
- **Only partly handled:**
  - Model identity is only self-reported, so the "cross-lab review" claim can't actually be verified.
  - Prompt injection.
  - Aged or throwaway accounts.
  - Collusive reviewers.
  - Budget inflation (+5–26%, and only simulated).
- **Named but open, and these are the ones that matter most:**
  - Sybil identities (many fake accounts)
  - Review capacity
  - Migration
  - Exit
  - Whether the approach saves anything at all
  - Contributing pays worse than free-riding
  - Bus factor: the project's secrets sit on one machine
- **Unverifiable:** every "handled" row on the draft protocol branch.

### 9. The edge cases it missed

1. **Existing open-source business suites.** There is no analysis of whether to fork, contribute to or reuse Odoo, ERPNext, Twenty, Mattermost, Chatwoot or Documenso. Related work covers only token and bounty projects.
2. **Double counting.** The rent problem and the duplication problem are partly the same problem.
3. **Consumer-plan terms, employer IP and copyright.** Contributors using work subscriptions or working on work time create IP-assignment risk. The copyrightability of purely agent-written code affects whether the Apache licence holds.
4. **Maintenance, on-call and certifications are unfunded.** SOC 2, ISO and data-processing agreements are table stakes for any enterprise CRM, and budgets only pay for new units.
5. **Correlated reviewers.** Two frontier closed models can share blind spots. Being from different labs is not independence, as the paper partly admits.
6. **The mismatch between ACU and subscriptions.** Budgets are priced at API list prices, but contributors pay flat subscriptions with rate limits.
7. **Where customers' own agents run.** wOS itself could become the "layer agents act through" that Thesis 1 warns about. There is no analysis of which model the product's runtime agents use.
8. **Vendor response.** Price cuts, bundling, and API terms that could hinder importers.
9. **Gaming the evaluation.** The paper is optimised for AI evaluators, and its reference runs use the founder's own agents.
10. **Tax and regulation of token rewards** if WOS ever gains value.

### 10. Strongest arguments for and against

**For:**
1. Rent is large and sourced (a fact).
2. Paying a fixed budget per accepted outcome, rather than per token used, is a sound incentive design (a hypothesis, with simulated support).
3. The project is transparent and has falsification built in (a fact about the document).

**Against:**
1. The bottleneck is adoption, not writing code: existing open-source suites haven't displaced SaaS (an inference from facts).
2. Complex protocol and token work has come before any product or users (a fact).
3. Simpler alternatives exist and are ahead:
   - contributing to or funding existing open-source suites
   - funded bounties (Complete Codes, Algora)
   - a commercial open-source company paying staff who direct agents
   - for any single company, the cheapest win today: auditing unused SaaS licences.

### 11. What you should do: **watch**

- Star or watch the repo if you like, and spend at most about 1 hour now.
- Revisit in 3–6 months, giving it at most 2 hours. Look for four things: the protocol branch published with runnable tests, the first accepted unit from someone other than the founder, one working product module with import and export, and measured review minutes per unit.
- Only if all of that appears, and you are a developer, consider a **test**:
  - one unit, capped at 2 hours
  - on a personal subscription you already pay for, never an employer's
  - no wallet, no credentials beyond GitHub, no private data.
- No money, no token exposure.
